## Honest verdict on the GEV pitch

Measured against the 20 winners, the GEV work is strong in one area and behind in the areas that decided both the judging and the outcomes.

Where it is ahead: it models the backlog as a flow. That means conversion by year, split by the year each order was signed, with a physical GW cross-check and a backtest against a year it did not use. No winner in the set did this, and it is exactly where Vestas, Baker Hughes and Motorola broke. The proof method is the most common winning one: reading the filings closely plus arithmetic on public data. It also uses the opponent's own 10-11 point figure as a ceiling. The rebuttal style is a method audit of a named report, which is the style that was right on its specific point. It would likely survive hostile questions about GLJ better than most winners survived questions about their own theses.

Where it is behind:

(1) It does not yet have a variant view. The lead rebuts GLJ, an outlier at $470 against a $962.49 price. Proving an outlier wrong puts the pitch on the same side as consensus. No team number sits next to a consensus number on a named line for a named year. At 38.7x forward earnings, about twice the market, a long on consensus numbers is a pure bet on the multiple. That is the Valvoline 2024 pattern: a winner at a premium multiple with consensus-like numbers, down 31% while the S&P rose 51%. It is also the Carlisle 2025 pattern: the business delivered, the multiple fell, and the stock is down about 11%. The corpus's own price data show GEV already fell about 15% from late April to late September 2026.

(2) The one measured gap to consensus points the other way. Street FY28 EBITDA sits about 17% above management's own framework. Left unresolved beside a long recommendation, it undercuts the pitch.

(3) The recommended lead has an untested rival explanation. Power segment margin blends services and equipment. It can rise on services mix alone without saying anything about the margin on the old equipment book, which is GLJ's actual claim.

(4) There is zero primary research. About 13-14 of 20 winners did some, including every Pershing winner. Primary research rarely carried the variance. Two of the three winners that skipped it leaned on a price-implied physical number instead, and this pitch has no reverse DCF either.

(5) The proof arrives late. The wedge concerns FY27 results, reported in full around January 2028, outside a 12-month horizon. Q3 prints after the finals. No in-horizon proof point, pass mark, kill rule or bear case with a lower multiple has been written.

(6) The process leaned heavily on AI tooling. No winner in the set presented AI-built evidence, and Citadel bans AI-generated content. The team has to own every key number by hand.

Bottom line: today this is a well-evidenced rebuttal, not yet a pitch with an edge. Judges asked winners one question more than any other: where do you differ from the Street, by how much, and why does that justify the multiple? This pitch would currently struggle with it. Two days is enough to fix the structure: the variant line, the direction rule, the rival-explanation split, a bear case with a lower multiple, a reverse DCF, dated proof points, a kill rule, and model checks. It is not enough to build the primary research base the Pershing winners had. Public utility regulatory filings, peer disclosures and an investor-relations call on definitions are the realistic substitutes.

## Tests a wedge must pass

1. Line-item test: the wedge changes a named model line, against a named and sourced consensus number, for a named year. If it changes no line, it is context, not a wedge.
2. Consensus-side test: the view being rebutted is what the price reflects (consensus or the dominant narrative), not an outlier. Proving an outlier wrong puts you on consensus's side and creates no variance.
3. Distance test: state the key-year gap to consensus in percent. Gaps of roughly 7-22% on one line survived judge Q&A among student winners; near-zero gaps mean the call is really about the multiple or duration and must say so.
4. Ratio test: divide target return by the estimate gap. If the target is several times the gap, most of the return is a multiple change, and it needs its own evidence (own history, peers, implied growth) and a dated trigger.
5. Physical-quantity test: the price (reverse DCF) or the consensus translates into one physical or operating quantity that can be checked against its own history or a hard ceiling in seconds.
6. Rival-explanation test: list the other variables (price vs volume, mix, cost, capex, multiple) that could explain the same price or the same reported trend, and show why yours is the one that moves.
7. Error-band test (derived from the independent-predictor cases): if your wedge rests on a model backtested against reported numbers, the backtest error must be smaller than the variance you claim.
8. Why-missed test: name a structural information gap a reader can verify (undisclosed segment profit, a dropped or switched KPI, a private competitor, a company-commissioned study). Then check the key input is not already in an earnings call or a sell-side note, as ASML's key figure was.
9. Already-priced test: check whether the stock has already moved on the same story, and how much of that move remains, before claiming the market has not seen it.
10. Cash test: the wedge moves EBIT, EBITDA or free cash flow, not only a label or classification. If reported numbers stay unchanged, every earnings print argues against you.
11. Reveal-date test: a company-reported number, on a known date inside the 3-12 month horizon, with a pass/fail level set in advance. Catalysts owned by a regulator, a policy date or an event with no pass mark tended to stall.
12. Counter-move test: what can management, customers or competitors do (buy a company instead of buying back stock, cut capex to protect margins, re-lock a channel)? Stress each separately.
13. Multiple-consistency test: the exit multiple fits the growth your own thesis implies, and the bear case lowers the multiple to a level anchored on the stock's own past drawdowns or trough multiples.
14. Kill test: a written, measurable condition (a reported metric crossing a level by a date) under which you are wrong.
15. Noise test: every risk you dismiss has a written observation that would prove it is not noise.
