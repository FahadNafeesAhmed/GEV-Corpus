What the thesis says and does not say

  • It says: analysts' earnings are backed by signed price and signed demand, so the return survives a lower multiple.
  • It says: the risk is factory output, and it can be watched in one reported number each quarter.
  • It does not say analysts' numbers are too low. Three attempts at that claim failed their error tests (section 11).
  • It does not prove 25x. The multiple is a judgment (section 7.6).

2. The company

  • Three segments. Power: gas turbines (plus nuclear, hydro and steam), sold as equipment and then serviced for decades. Electrification: grid equipment such as transformers and switchgear. Wind: onshore and offshore turbines. [S1]
  • Spun out of General Electric in April 2024. GE Aerospace is a separate company. [S17]
  • Prolec GE: the remaining 50% was bought on 2 February 2026 for $5,254M in cash. It adds about $3.1B of Electrification revenue in FY2026. [S3, S6]

3. The industry

3.1 Demand

  • Orders have outrun the industry's ability to build. Wood Mackenzie puts world orders at 110 GW at the end of 2025 against capacity of 60 to 70 GW a year. [S13, S14]
  • Wood Mackenzie expects turbine orders to peak in 2026, and turbine prices to reach about $600 per kW by the end of 2027, up 195% since 2019. [S13]
  • Makers disagree on how long it lasts. Mitsubishi Heavy expects about 70 GW a year on average over five years and does not assume 100 GW. Siemens Energy's CEO has spoken of up to 120 GW a year. [S12, S15]
  • Data-centre customers are reported at about 20% of GEV's contracted gas volume. Trade press; one source. [S17]

3.2 Supply

  • Three firms build large gas turbines at scale: GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi Heavy. [S17]
  • Reported backlogs: GEV 116 GW (53 firm plus 63 reserved); Siemens Energy 69 GW firm; Mitsubishi 35 GW of large-frame units. The three do not count the same things. [S6, S15]
  • One industry estimate has world capacity rising from 67 GW to 102 GW a year by 2030, with the three large makers adding about 28 GW of that. One source. [S20]
  • Mitsubishi says it can lift capacity by 30% by FY2028. [S12]
  • GEV's lead time is about three years, and about 10 GW of slots were left across 2029 and 2030 in April 2026. By July it was mostly sold out through 2030, with over half of 2031 expected to be contracted by year end. [S22, S9]

4. The gigawatt analysis

4.1 The identity

  • Power equipment revenue = gas turbine gigawatts shipped x revenue per kW. One GW is 1,000,000 kW, so GW x $/kW gives $ millions.
  • GEV reports gigawatts and units ordered and sold in the Power table of each 10-K and 10-Q. [S1, S3]
  • Every per-kW figure here is all-in. It includes aeroderivative units, steam turbines and non-gas equipment (estimated at about $1.2B a year, not disclosed), and it moves with product mix.
  • Revenue on customised equipment is recognised over time, so GW times price is an approximation. [S1]

4.10 What this analysis supports

  • The Street's Power equipment path equals the company's stated output at prices already signed. It needs no new orders and no price above the signed book.
  • What is left to deliver it is output and conversion, not demand.
  • The units that ship from mid-2026 are mostly the newer orders.
  • It does not show that the Street is too low for any period (section 11).

5. Manufacturing: why output is the limit

5.1 Why blades are the limit

  • First-stage blades and vanes sit in gas hotter than the melting point of their own metal. They survive through internal cooling channels, ceramic coatings and single-crystal casting. [S17, S16]
  • Single-crystal blades are costly to cast and have low yield. [S26]
  • Western production is concentrated in four firms: Precision Castparts, Howmet, Consolidated Precision Products (CPP) and Doncasters. They also supply jet engines. [S21]
  • Howmet and Precision Castparts are the two dominant producers; Howmet is reported at about half of the market for gas turbine components. [S16]
  • Wood Mackenzie calls single-crystal blade production the industry's critical bottleneck, done at scale by a handful of suppliers. [S13]

5.2 What management says (Q2 2026 call, 22 July 2026)

Statement, paraphrasedSource
Output is moving from about three gigawatts a quarter to five from the third quarter, and that is on track for both Q3 and Q4.[S9]

GEV went to its castings and forgings suppliers in summer 2024 and paid for some new furnaces. Reported from a May 2026 conference; not a filing.[S16]

5.3 Who else wants the same castings

  • GE Aerospace agreed in September 2026 to buy CPP for $11.75B, closing in the second half of 2027. CPP makes castings for aircraft engines and for industrial gas turbines. [S17]
  • SpaceX is reported to be building its own foundry for gas turbine blades and vanes in Texas, because casting capacity is so tight. [S16]
  • Whether CPP supplies GEV is not disclosed.

7.2 Method

  • Forward EBITDA at 30 September 2027 = 25% of FY2027 + 75% of FY2028.
  • Enterprise value = exit multiple x forward EBITDA.
  • Equity value = enterprise value + net cash.
  • Price = equity value / diluted shares.
  • Calendar weights. GEV earns more in the fourth quarter, so this overstates forward EBITDA by less than 1%.
